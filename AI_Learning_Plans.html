§The premise, with two corrections

The interview doesn't test what you've read

It tests whether you can build something useful on top of a component that is non-deterministic, expensive, and occasionally confidently wrong — and whether you can prove it works.

Correction one. Most people prepare for this by consuming AI content: newsletters, launch threads, model benchmarks. That produces someone who can name things. The interview is looking for someone who has felt the actual problems — a retrieval step that returns plausible-but-wrong chunks, an agent that loops twelve times and burns $4, a prompt change that fixes one case and silently breaks nine. You only get those scars from building. So this plan is 85% building, 15% reading, and the reading is structured so it feeds the building rather than replacing it.

Correction two — this one is specific to you. You currently get code from an assistant. That is the correct way to work in 2026 and nobody will penalise it. But it creates one precise exposure: the interview tests what the assistant cannot do for you. Deciding what to build. Recognising when the output is wrong before you run it. Defending a line of code when someone asks "what happens if this is null?" Every phase below ends in something you have to explain, and the weekly cadence has a standing cold rep — a timed build with the assistant closed. Not because assistant-free coding is virtuous, but because the ability to read and judge generated code is downstream of having written that code yourself at least once.

What "AI-fluent" actually means to an interviewer

Not "has used ChatGPT." It means: you can take a product requirement, decide whether an LLM belongs in it at all, pick the cheapest architecture that meets the bar, put a number on quality, and say what happens when it fails. Five things. The whole plan is those five things.

Language: Python — decided

Every provider's SDK, every eval harness, every retrieval library, and nearly all the reference code you'll read is Python-first. You do not need to be strong at Python — you need to be able to read it fluently and write ~200-line services in it. That is a two-week ramp alongside Phase 0 and 1, not a prerequisite.

If your product surface is a web app, write the thin serving layer in TypeScript later — the SDKs mirror each other closely and the concepts transfer unchanged. Don't start there: the ecosystem you'll be reading is Python, and translating while learning doubles the cost.

This decision is closed. Switching at Week 6 costs more than any difference between the two.

00PhaseWeek 1 · Oct 5–11

The floor

Not a technique. The substrate every later argument sits on.

0/0

Concepts

Do

Week 1 deliverable

A ~60-line script: takes a question, calls the model, prints the answer and input tokens, output tokens, and cost in cents. Every later phase reuses this cost print. If you can't see what a call costs, you will not reason about cost.

The cost math, worked once

Do this arithmetic by hand now and it stays with you. A document-Q&A request carrying 8,000 tokens of retrieved context and returning 600 tokens, on a mid-tier model at $2/MTok in and $10/MTok out:

ComponentTokensRate /MTokCost
Input (prompt + 8k context)8,000$2.00$0.0160
Output600$10.00$0.0060
Per request8,600—$0.022
At 50k requests/day——$1,100/day

Now redo it on a top-tier model at $5/$25: $0.055/request, $2,750/day. That 2.5× is the entire substance of "which model should we use", and being able to produce this table live is a genuine differentiator in a design round.

Rates above are illustrative, current to roughly mid-2026 and falling steadily — always price from the provider's live pricing page, never from memory. The method is the durable part.

Why this is first

RAG, caching, agent design, and model selection are all arguments about what goes in the context window and what it costs. Skip this week and every later topic becomes vocabulary instead of reasoning.

01PhaseWeeks 2–3 · Oct 12–25

The API surface, properly

Everything a production call needs that a tutorial call doesn't. This is where "I've used an LLM" becomes "I've shipped one."

0/0

Core

Prompting — as engineering, not incantation

Budget three sessions here, not three weeks. Prompting is real and it is also the most overhyped and fastest-decaying skill in this plan: tricks that mattered on 2023 models are noise or actively harmful on current ones. What persists:

  • Specificity over cleverness. State the task, the audience, the format, and the edge cases. Most "prompt engineering" is just writing an unambiguous spec.
  • Examples beat adjectives. Two or three worked examples outperform a paragraph describing what you want.
  • Make the output contract explicit — and then enforce it with structured output rather than hope.
  • Give the model an out. "If the context doesn't contain the answer, say so" removes a large slice of hallucinations in RAG.
  • Prompts are code. They go in version control, they get reviewed, they get an eval. A prompt without an eval is a change you cannot verify.
Phase deliverable

One HTTP endpoint that does a real extraction or classification job: strict JSON schema out, streaming where it helps, retry-with-backoff, a hard timeout, prompt caching on the stable prefix, and a structured log line per request carrying input_tokens, output_tokens, cost_usd, latency_ms.

That log line is the spine of Phases 4 and 5. Build it now.

Trap

Do not pick up an orchestration framework yet. Frameworks are abstractions over exactly the mechanics above — adopt one before you know the mechanics and you'll be debugging someone else's indirection with no mental model of what's underneath. Interviewers can tell in about two questions.

02PhaseWeeks 4–5 · Oct 26 – Nov 8

Retrieval

Giving the model facts it wasn't trained on. The most commonly built, most commonly broken, and most commonly asked-about AI feature there is.

0/0

Concepts

Why RAG fails — be able to list these cold

FailureSymptomFix
Retrieval missThe answer exists in the corpus; the right chunk was never fetchedHybrid search, query rewriting, raise k then rerank
Bad chunk boundaryRight document, wrong fragment — context cut mid-table or mid-clauseStructure-aware chunking, overlap, larger chunks + rerank
Context stuffing20 chunks in, quality down, cost upFewer, better chunks. More context is not more signal
Stale indexConfidently cites a deleted or superseded policyRe-index pipeline, document versioning, show dates
No abstentionInvents an answer when the corpus genuinely lacks oneExplicit "say you don't know" instruction + eval cases that test it
The question you'll be asked: "why not just put everything in a 1M-token context?"

Sometimes you should — for a single large document, long-context plus prompt caching beats a retrieval pipeline on both accuracy and engineering cost. It stops working when: the corpus exceeds the window; you're paying for 1M tokens on every request to use 3k of them; latency matters; or you need permission filtering per user. The strong answer isn't "RAG is better" — it's naming the crossover point. That nuance is worth more than any implementation detail.

Phase deliverable

RAG over a corpus you actually own — your notes, your team's docs, a repo's markdown. Hybrid retrieval, a reranking step, citations in the response.

Then the part almost nobody does: hand-write 30 question → expected-source pairs and measure recall@5. One number. Change the chunk size, measure again. You now have something to say in an interview that 90% of candidates cannot say.

03PhaseWeeks 6–7 · Nov 9–22

Agents and tool use

Letting the model drive a loop. Genuinely powerful, massively over-applied, and the area where showing restraint scores higher than showing enthusiasm.

0/0

Build the loop by hand first

Before any framework, write this yourself — it is about forty lines and it is the entire concept:

Tool design — where the quality actually is

Then: MCP, and the four-question gate

Ask these four before building any agent — out loud, in the interview

Complexity — is the task genuinely multi-step and hard to specify in advance, or is it a fixed pipeline wearing a costume? Value — does the outcome justify the latency and cost of a loop? Viability — is the model actually good at this task? Cost of error — can mistakes be caught and reversed (tests, review, rollback)?

A "no" anywhere means use a single call or a deterministic pipeline instead. Reaching for an agent when a switch statement would do is the most common senior-level red flag in this field. The candidate who says "I'd start with one structured call and only add a loop if the task shape demands it" outscores the one who reaches for a multi-agent swarm.

Phase deliverable

An agent over your Phase 2 retrieval project with three or four real tools (search the corpus, fetch a record, perform one write action behind a confirmation). Hard turn and cost caps. A trace log that records every turn: tool called, arguments, result, tokens, cumulative cost.

Then deliberately break it. Give it an impossible task and watch it loop. Give it an ambiguous one and watch it pick the wrong tool. Write down what happened — those two paragraphs are your answer to "tell me about a time an AI system failed on you."

04PhaseWeeks 8–9 · Nov 23 – Dec 6

Evals

The highest-leverage two weeks in this plan. If you only get good at one thing, get good at this one.

0/0

Here is the asymmetry worth exploiting: nearly every candidate can describe RAG, and most can describe an agent loop. Very few can describe how they knew their change made things better. In a non-deterministic system you cannot ship on vibes — a prompt tweak that fixes the case in front of you routinely breaks three you aren't looking at. The eval is the regression test, and treating it as the centre of the discipline rather than an afterthought is the clearest available signal of seniority.

Building an eval set

Grading — in order of preference

MethodUse whenWatch for
ProgrammaticThere is a checkable fact: valid JSON, correct label, required field present, the right document citedNothing. Always prefer this. Cheap, fast, deterministic
HumanCalibrating anything else, and for the first 20 cases of any new evalExpensive and slow — so spend it on calibration, not volume
LLM-as-judgeOpen-ended quality where no programmatic check existsPosition bias, verbosity bias, self-preference. Must be validated against human labels before you trust it. Give it a rubric, not "is this good?"

Running them

Phase deliverable

An eval suite over your Phase 2–3 project. 40+ cases, mixed graders, a one-command run that prints score, cost, and p95 latency.

Then use it: make three real changes (a chunking change, a model change, a prompt change) and record what each did to all three numbers. Write it up as a one-page report with a before/after table. That page is the most persuasive artefact you can bring to an interview — more than the project itself.

05PhaseWeek 10 · Dec 7–13

Production — cost, latency, safety

The questions a staff engineer asks in the second half of a design round.

0/0

Cost levers, in the order you should apply them

Free wins before tradeoffs — this ordering is itself the answer to "the AI bill is too high, what do you do?"

  1. Prompt caching. Usually the largest single win and it costs no quality. Verify it's actually hitting — a cache-read count stuck at zero means a silent invalidator in your prefix.
  2. Input hygiene. Stop sending what the model doesn't need. Retrieve 5 good chunks instead of 20 mediocre ones; trim boilerplate; prune dead conversation history.
  3. Output hygiene. Output tokens cost several times input tokens. Ask for the answer, not the answer plus a preamble plus a summary.
  4. Batch anything that isn't latency-sensitive, for roughly half price.
  5. Reasoning effort. Dial thinking depth down on routine traffic, up where correctness pays. Tune per route, not globally.
  6. Model choice, last. Route easy traffic to a small model and hard traffic to a large one — but measure first: a top model at low effort often beats a smaller model at high effort, and a single model keeps one cache namespace instead of fragmenting it.

06PhaseWeek 11 · Dec 14–20

Working with an assistant, under examination

You already generate code with an assistant. This week is about making that a demonstrable strength instead of an unexamined dependency.

0/0

Interview loops in 2026 split roughly three ways, and you should be ready for all three. Some ban assistants outright and watch you write code cold. Some hand you an assistant and watch how you use it — that's the increasingly common one. And some give you AI-generated code and ask you to review it. The third is the hardest and the most revealing.

Drills

The workflow to demonstrate when an assistant is allowed

Narrate it; don't just do it silently.

  1. Restate the problem and name the edge cases before writing anything. This is scored whether or not AI is involved.
  2. Write the test or the signature first — the contract comes from you.
  3. Delegate the implementation with a specific prompt.
  4. Review the output critically, out loud. "I'd want to check what this does with an empty input" is a sentence that earns points.
  5. Run it, then explain it. Own every line as if you'd typed it.
The sentence that ends interviews

"I'm not sure, the assistant wrote that part." Generated code is yours the moment you submit it. The whole purpose of this week is to never need that sentence.

07PhaseWeek 12 · Dec 21–27

Interview consolidation

No new material. Turning twelve weeks of building into answers you can give under pressure.

0/0

AI system design — a scaffold that fits almost any prompt

"Design a support assistant over our help centre." "Design a code-review bot." "Design document Q&A over 10M PDFs." Same six moves every time:

  1. Clarify and scope. Who uses it, what volume, what's the cost of a wrong answer, what latency is acceptable? Ask about the cost of being wrong — most candidates don't, and it determines the entire architecture.
  2. Do the arithmetic. Requests/day × tokens/request × rate = daily spend. Out loud. This is the move that separates the top of the stack from the middle.
  3. Pick the simplest architecture that could work. Single call → fixed pipeline → retrieval → agent, in that order, and justify each escalation. Start simpler than feels impressive.
  4. State the eval plan before the implementation details. "Here's how I'd know it works" is a differentiator, and almost nobody volunteers it unprompted.
  5. Failure and abuse. What happens when the model is wrong, the API is down, a user is hostile, a retrieved document is poisoned?
  6. Rollout. Shadow mode → internal users → % rollout, with the production signal you'd watch at each gate.

The question bank — be able to answer all of these in under two minutes each

  1. Why do models hallucinate, and what actually reduces it? Wanted: grounding, abstention instructions, citations, constrained output — and the admission that it cannot be eliminated.
  2. Walk me through a RAG pipeline you've built. Where did it break? Your Phase 2 project. The breakage is the interesting half — lead with it.
  3. How do you know your prompt change made things better? Phase 4. If you have a real before/after table, this question is a gift.
  4. When would you not use an agent? The four-question gate. Restraint scores.
  5. Our AI bill tripled. What do you look at? The lever ordering from Phase 5, free wins first.
  6. How do you stop prompt injection? Architectural defence, least privilege. Say plainly that prompt-level filtering is not a solution.
  7. Fine-tuning vs RAG vs prompting — pick one for this problem. Prompting for behaviour, RAG for knowledge, fine-tuning for format/style/latency at scale. Most problems are not fine-tuning problems.
  8. How do you pick a model? Eval on your own task, then cost and latency. Public benchmarks are a shortlist, not a decision.
  9. This feature needs a sub-second response. How? Streaming, smaller model, caching, precompute, cut output tokens, parallelise.
  10. How would you handle 10M documents instead of 10k? Ingestion pipeline, incremental re-indexing, ANN index choice, metadata pre-filtering, cost of re-embedding.
  11. What's the most interesting thing happening in AI right now? Your Track B log. Three specific, dated, opinionated answers. Never "AGI is coming."
  12. Where have you seen AI applied badly? Tests judgment and honesty. Have a real example and be specific about why.

The map — what to be able to place, not memorise

Specific numbers here go stale in weeks. The structure doesn't. Learn the layers; look up the current occupants when you need them.

LayerWhat to know
Frontier labsThe main closed-weight families (Anthropic's Claude, OpenAI's GPT, Google's Gemini) and the main open-weight ones (Meta's Llama, Mistral, and the strong Chinese labs — DeepSeek, Qwen). Know roughly who leads at what, and hold that opinion loosely: the ordering changes every few months.
Open vs closedThe real tradeoff: control, data residency and per-token cost against peak capability and zero ops. "When would you self-host an open model?" is a common question — the honest answer is usually data-residency requirements or very high steady volume, not cost at small scale.
Where inference runsFirst-party APIs; the cloud resellers (AWS Bedrock, Google Vertex, Microsoft Foundry) where your company's existing contract usually lives; inference specialists competing on speed and price; and local runtimes (Ollama, llama.cpp) for private or offline work.
The tooling layerOrchestration frameworks, vector databases, eval and observability platforms, and MCP as the emerging interop standard. Know what category each thing is in. You do not need to have used them all — you need to not be surprised by a name.
The economicsTraining is enormous capex; inference is the recurring opex you actually manage. Price per token has fallen steadily and keeps falling; capability per dollar is the metric that matters. This is why "we'll just use the biggest model" is a losing position and why model routing exists.
The shape of progressLonger contexts, reasoning/thinking modes with tunable depth, native tool use, multimodality, and agentic long-horizon work. Each of these changed what's buildable — be able to say what each one unlocked in product terms.
Policy and governanceThe EU AI Act, data-retention and zero-retention terms, model cards and safety evaluations, copyright and training-data disputes. One paragraph each is enough. It matters more the more regulated the employer.

§Operating rhythm

Weekly cadence

About one hour a day, six days. Both tracks fit inside it.

DayFocusShape
Mon–WedNew material20 min reading the concept, 40 min writing code against it. Never read for a full hour — a concept you haven't typed is a concept you don't have.
ThuExtend the projectFold the week's new idea into the one running project. No new repos; one system that grows for twelve weeks is worth far more than eight demos.
FriCold rep + review drill45 min building something small with the assistant closed. Then 15 min generating a function and finding its bugs before running it. Both halves matter.
SatMeasureRun the eval (from Week 8 on). Record score, cost, p95. Write one line on what changed and why. Before Week 8, use Saturday to catch up or to deepen the week's weakest topic.
SunTrack BThe 45-minute ritual, hard-capped, ending in three written sentences.

Four rules that do most of the work

  1. One project, twelve weeks. Everything compounds into the same system. Eight tutorials teach eight beginnings; one system that survives a retrieval rewrite, an agent loop and an eval suite teaches what the beginnings never do.
  2. Build it raw before you adopt an abstraction. Hand-rolled agent loop before any framework, plain vector search before a vector DB. You can adopt the abstraction afterwards in an afternoon, and you'll choose it on merit.
  3. Every claim gets a number. "It got better" is not a result. "Recall@5 went 0.71 → 0.84, cost per request down 18%" is. Train this now, because it's how you'll be expected to speak in the interview.
  4. Write down every failure. A one-line log of what broke and why. By Week 12 it's your answer bank for the behavioural half of the loop, which you would otherwise have to improvise.
§Failure modes

Traps that will cost weeks

TrapWhy it's expensive
Framework before fundamentalsLearn an orchestration library before you've hand-written a tool loop and you inherit someone else's abstractions with no model of what's underneath. Debugging becomes archaeology, and an interviewer finds the gap in two questions.
Collecting demosEight half-finished projects demonstrate eight first days. One system carried through retrieval, agents, evals and deployment demonstrates engineering.
Reading as a substitute for buildingConsuming AI content produces fluent-sounding vocabulary with no judgment underneath, and interviewers are now well calibrated to detect exactly that. It's 15% of this plan for a reason.
Treating prompting as the skillIt's real, it's small, and it decays fastest. Techniques that mattered two model generations ago are now noise. Architecture, evals and cost reasoning don't decay.
Reaching for an agentThe most common senior-level red flag in the field. A loop where a single structured call would do means more cost, more latency and more failure modes. Restraint reads as experience.
Skipping evalsPhase 4 is the differentiator and it's also the most skippable-feeling phase because nothing visible gets built. Skip it and you'll be one of the many candidates who can describe a system but not verify one.
Shipping without numbers"It works well" is unfalsifiable and everyone says it. A quality score, a cost per request and a p95 are what make a project credible in four seconds.
Memorising the newsParameter counts and benchmark scores are stale in weeks and no one is asking. A map you can place new things on is durable; a list you memorised isn't.
Not owning generated codeSubmitting code you can't defend line by line is the fastest way to lose a loop you were otherwise passing. Phase 6 exists entirely for this.
